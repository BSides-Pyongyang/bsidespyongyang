An Uninvited Delegation from DPRK: Inside the Binance Hack

Andrew MacPherson

Abstract

DPRK used Binance’s own signatures to keep stealing after withdrawals were shut down. I’ll trace the attack from a developer’s interview assignment into the signing infrastructure, explain why one transaction contained fifty authorizations, and show how EIP-7702 gave the attacker access that survived the shutdown. I’ll also walk through the earlier testing I found on-chain and the limits of what I could connect to the heist. It’s a look at what those signatures actually authorized, how the attacker used them, and what recovering control of the accounts requires.

How to Steal Six Billion Dollars: The DPRK's Cryptocurrency Acquisition Framework

himug-lamuh

Abstract

Stealing billions of dollars is difficult.

Stealing billions of dollars repeatedly, across multiple years, against increasingly prepared defenders is considerably more impressive.

This session examines how cryptocurrency operations attributed to the Democratic People's Republic of Korea appear to have evolved from opportunistic exchange compromises into the world's most effective cyber-enabled revenue programs. Rather than focusing on a single incident, we'll trace the evolution of tactics across nearly a decade of publicly attributed operations, following the progression from technical exploitation to long-term social engineering, organizational compromise, industrialized laundering, and operational specialization.

Presented as a very enthusiastic annual shareholder meeting celebrating another year of patriotic innovation, this talk combines public reporting, blockchain investigations, and incident postmortems to explore what filthy capitalists (defenders) can learn from an adversary that continuously adapts faster than much of the cryptocurrency ecosystem.

Because while the cryptocurrency industry spent years asking, "How do we stop the next hack?", the elite cyber heroes of Pyongyang already perfected the business of running them.

lazarusholic

Abstract

I'm a huge fan of Lazarus and friends. To me, they are BTS.

The rules are simple: public reports only. What began as copy-pasting reports into a spreadsheet is now run by a team of AI agents built on Hermes Agent: a collector that hunts new reports, an assistant that extracts and structures them, and a lastweek writer that publishes the weekly digest.

The fan club archive now holds 3,900+ reports, 250+ threat actor code names, 220+ incidents, and 33,000+ IOCs. I'll show how 250+ code names collapse into 8 actual groups, quantify how much confusion the industry's naming habits create, and trace how the group's world tour shifted from disruption and espionage to financial theft.

This is a personal side project. Lazarus made billions. But I did not.

Talk | OSINT Block

Ship Happens: Detection Engineering for DPRK Maritime Sanctions Evasion

Ian Gillespie

Abstract

My previous research has focused on DPRK cyber operations and the laundering of stolen cryptocurrency, but cyber-enabled revenue is only one side of the problem: North Korea must ultimately convert money and access into physical goods, fuel, and logistics.

This talk explores what happens when threat-intelligence and detection-engineering techniques are applied to maritime sanctions evasion.

I built an OSINT platform that ingests live AIS, satellite AIS-derived maritime data, sanctions information, geospatial zones, and vessel identity history to flag behaviors associated with DPRK maritime activity: ship-to-ship transfers, AIS dark periods, identity manipulation, and movement consistent with DPRK-bound routing. It applies concepts familiar to security practitioners—telemetry collection, IOC enrichment, rule-based detection, scoring, suppression, and sensor-health monitoring to a very different operational environment.

The talk will also cover how real-world testing shaped the platform: stale telemetry initially created false encounter durations, overly broad geofences turned busy harbors into apparent “transfers,” a sanctions filter admitted unrelated UN-listed entities, and an AIS feed outage made our own blind spot look like vessels “going dark.” Each issue drove changes to the detection logic, health monitoring, and false-positive controls, and the talk will show what the platform surfaces once those safeguards are in place.

Rather than presenting maritime data as definitive, I’ll show how corroboration, provenance, and suppression turn noisy signals into defensible investigative leads, where satellite radar imagery fits in, and what cybersecurity researchers can learn from threat hunting when the endpoints are ships instead of computers.

npm install --dprk: DeceptiveDevelopment’s Supply-Chain Playbooks Against Developers

Ettore Bordoni

Abstract

DPRK-linked DeceptiveDevelopment continues to target software developers by exploiting trust in modern development workflows. Based on multiple investigations and telemetry, this talk examines the group’s evolving use of malicious npm packages and its expansion into PyPI and Go.

We will analyze multi-stage infection chains triggered by routine actions such as installing dependencies, opening coding challenges, or working inside attacker-controlled repositories. These chains use installation scripts, host fingerprinting, transitive dependency abuse, public dead drops, Git repositories, IPFS, and legitimate services to stage and retrieve payloads.

The talk will also explore the actor’s use of blockchain infrastructure for payload and C2 retrieval, including EtherHiding, NullReceiver, TRON, and Aptos. We will demonstrate how hunting across public Google Docs can uncover related packages, repositories, infrastructure, and campaign activity.

Finally, we will examine SSH key backdooring, OpenClaw configuration theft, several associated malware families, and Telegram as a backup exfiltration channel. Attendees will gain practical techniques for pivoting across package metadata, dependency graphs, repositories, public documents, endpoints, network telemetry, and blockchain activity.

Talk

The Equation Group: A Russian Perspective on America’s APT

Thanatos

Abstract

Equation Group is widely regarded as one of the most sophisticated cyber espionage groups ever observed, with public reporting linking its capabilities to the U.S. intelligence community and NSA's Tailored Access Operations (TAO). From EquationDrug and DoubleFantasy to Fanny, GrayFish, and capabilities later exposed through the Shadow Brokers, Equation demonstrated an extraordinary combination of exploit development, persistence, operational security, and intelligence collection.

In this presentation, a Russian military cyber operator shares publicly available research and hard-earned lessons about Equation Group with our colleagues and friends in the Democratic People's Republic of Korea. We will examine its publicly documented toolsets, suspected relationship with NSA/TAO, connections to operations such as Stuxnet and Flame, and the defensive and operational lessons that can be learned from America's most capable cyber adversary.
